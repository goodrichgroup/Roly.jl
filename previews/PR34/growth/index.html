Bounded and unbounded rules

Some rule sets run out of structures and others grow forever. isbounded settles which.

The figures below use CairoMakie because the documentation builds headless. For 3D of your own, prefer GLMakie or WGLMakie, which depth-test instead of sorting.

A bounded system runs out

Squares whose site 1 binds site 2 can only turn the same way at every bond, so a chain of them folds back on itself.

ring = BindingRules([1 1 1 2], UnitSquare)
isbounded(ring)
true

true here is a proof rather than a search giving up. Any connected part of a valid structure is valid, and an infinite structure contains an infinite chain, so it is enough to grow chains one particle at a time. A chain long enough must repeat one of finitely many states, and the rigid motion between the two repeats can then be applied forever. Here that motion is a quarter turn, and every copy of a pure rotation stays the same distance from its axis, so infinitely many of them cannot fit. The whole system is therefore finite and polycount counts it exactly.

polycount(ring)
PolyformCount[n=4, exact, maxsize=4, Complete]

Four structures, and the largest closes into a ring with no open site left.

polys = polygen(ring)
biggest = polys[argmax(nparticles.(polys))]
nparticles(biggest), nbonds(biggest), length(opensitelocs(biggest))
(4, 4, 0)
render(biggest)

An unbounded system

Binding opposite sides of a square instead gives the square lattice.

square = BindingRules([1 2 1 4; 1 3 1 1], UnitSquare)
isbounded(square)
false

A single square repeats under a pure translation, which is the simplest way to be unbounded: every copy sits one step further along, so none of them ever meets another. The structures grow without limit.

maximum(nparticles, polygen(square; maxsize=6))
6
render(polygen(square; maxsize=6)[end])

Counting what cannot be enumerated

An unbounded system has infinitely many structures, so polycount needs a maxsize. Up to that size it enumerates exactly while the count is small, and switches to sampling the reverse-search tree beyond budget.

polycount(square; maxsize=9)
PolyformCount[n≈13600.0 ± 61.0, maxsize≥9, MaxSizeReached]

The ≈ and the error bar mark an estimate rather than a count.

Unbounded without ever repeating

In the plane, a repeated motion is a translation or a rotation, and only a translation escapes. In space a motion can be a screw, a turn about an axis together with a climb along it, and a screw escapes whether or not its turn ever adds up to a whole one. Regular tetrahedra are the standard example. Glued face to face they form the Boerdijk-Coxeter helix, whose twist per tetrahedron is acos(-2/3), an irrational fraction of a turn.

tetrahedra = PolyhedronParticleSpecies(Tetrahedron(); colors=fill(1, 4))
helixrules = BindingRules([1 1 1 1], tetrahedra)
isbounded(helixrules)
false

Copies far enough apart along the axis can never touch, which is what makes the growth infinite, and no multiple of the angle is ever a full turn, so the chain never closes. This is why isbounded looks at the repeating motion itself rather than for a periodic structure.

The helix is visible in the structures. Among the chains of eight tetrahedra, the longest reaches well short of eight straight steps, because it is curling.

function ischain(p)
    degree = zeros(Int, nparticles(p))
    for (a, b) in bonds(p)
        degree[a.particle] += 1
        degree[b.particle] += 1
    end
    return all(<=(2), degree)
end

chains = filter(ischain, polygen(helixrules; maxsize=8))
helix = chains[argmax(nparticles.(chains))]
xs = [p.pose.x for p in helix.particles]
norm(xs[end] - xs[1]) / (nparticles(helix) - 1)
0.31869981422524624
render(helix)
